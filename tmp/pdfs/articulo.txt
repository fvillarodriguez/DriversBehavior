Abstract
Accurate crash prediction is vital for road safety, but sustaining model performance over time remains
challenging due to evolving traffic conditions and shifting data distributions. Despite advances in developing predictive models, how and when to recalibrate these models has received limited attention in
crash prediction research. This study addresses this gap by evaluating four recalibration strategies: static,
period aligned, cumulative, and adaptive drift detection using ADWIN (Adaptive Windowing), applied to
crash prediction models (XGBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest, and NNet) using Automatic Vehicle Identification data from Santiago, Chile (2018–2024). Unlike time based approaches, ADWIN automatically
detects concept drift by monitoring prediction error distributions, enabling updates only when statistically
significant changes occur. Results show ensemble models, especially XGBoost and AdaBoost, offer the
best tradeoff between accuracy, calibration, and computational cost. Cumulative retraining emerges as
most effective, likely because crash prediction’s rare event nature benefits from maximizing historical data.
While ADWIN offers a theoretically appealing adaptive approach, its performance suggests drift detection
methods require further refinement for rare event contexts. These findings provide the first systematic
comparison of recalibration strategies for crash prediction, demonstrating that rare event characteristics
favor data accumulation over adaptive drift detection.
Keywords: Crash prediction, recalibration, drift detection, machine learning, predictive performance.

Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) have become an effective means to reduce traffic accidents
and improve the operational efficiency of urban highways (Ganin et al., 2019; Saleemi et al., 2022). By
integrating advanced technologies such as real-time sensors, vehicular networks, predictive analytics, and
machine learning algorithms (Sethupathy, 2022; Liu et al., 2024), ITS support data-driven decision-making
in road traffic management (Lilhore et al., 2022). A prominent example is real-time crash prediction
(RTCP), which uses statistical and machine learning models together with disaggregated traffic speed
and flow data to anticipate accident occurrence (Hossain et al., 2019). These systems enable the early
identification of crash-prone conditions and the deployment of preventive measures, including dynamic
alerts (Basso et al., 2021a, 2022b) and traffic flow adjustments (Silva et al., 2020), thereby helping to
reduce accident frequency and severity while enhancing safety in complex, dynamic urban settings (Ganin
et al., 2019; Jagatheesaperumal et al., 2024; Elassy et al., 2024). A wide range of machine learning and
deep learning techniques have been applied to crash prediction on highways, drawing on diverse data
sources such as loop detectors (Zhu et al., 2024), video cameras (Wang et al., 2023), speed radars, and
Automatic Vehicle Identification (AVI) systems (Ali et al., 2024).
Despite significant advancements, the performance of artificial intelligence (AI) techniques for crash
prediction can decline over time as changes in driving patterns, seasonal variations, regulatory updates, or
extraordinary events alter the underlying data distribution and weaken predictive accuracy (Kuhn et al.,
2013). In such dynamic contexts, recalibration is essential to sustain model effectiveness (Toubeau et al.,
2021; Kuleshov et al., 2018) and ensure continued value for real-time decision-making. This challenge is
particularly relevant for Autopista Central, a major urban highway in Santiago, Chile, where a pilot RTCP
system developed by Basso et al. (2018) is in operation. A key factor behind performance degradation
is drift, the gradual or abrupt change in the statistical properties of input or output variables over time,
which can disrupt the relationship between predictors and the target variable (Bifet and Gavalda, 2007).
While drift detection methods have shown effectiveness in other domains, they remain unexplored in this
context. More broadly, there is no prior research comparing alternative recalibration strategies within
operational online crash prediction systems, despite the need to sustain accuracy under evolving traffic
patterns while controlling computational demands.
In this paper, we address the challenge of recalibrating accident prediction models in non-stationary,
dynamic traffic environments, using multi-year real-world data (2018–2024) from Autopista Central in Santiago, Chile. The highway’s Automatic AVI gate system generates over 600 million records annually—more
than 1.6 million daily observations—placing substantial demands on data processing and model updating.
In such contexts, recalibration must balance sustaining predictive accuracy under evolving traffic patterns
with minimizing the computational time, resource use, and operational complexity of frequent updates. To
address this, we contribute to the literature by applying a set of recalibration strategies—static, periodaligned, cumulative, and adaptive via the ADWIN algorithm—to RTCP models built with XGBoost,
AdaBoost, Random Forest, and Neural Networks, using AVI data from Autopista Central. We then conduct extensive computational experiments to compare the efficiency and predictive performance of these
strategies, highlighting their respective advantages and limitations.
The structure of this article is as follows: Section 2 we position our investigation in the state of art with
a extensive literature review. Section 3 describes the characteristics of the dataset used, including data
sources, initial preprocessing, and the variables considered. Section 4 outlines the proposed methodology,
detailing the recalibration approaches evaluated, the integration of the ADWIN concept drift detector,
and the training procedures. Section 5 presents the computational experiments, the results obtained, and
a comparative analysis of the methodologies. Finally, Section 6 discusses the study’s conclusions and
proposes future research directions.

2. Literature Review
This section reviews the literature that supports this study. Specifically, Subsection 2.1 reviews recent
advances in crash prediction models, focusing on statistical and machine learning approaches for highways
and urban intersections. Subsection 2.2 discusses the challenges of maintaining model performance in
dynamic settings, with particular attention to data and concept drift. Subsection 2.3 presents applications of drift detection in transportation, highlighting their effectiveness and current limitations. Finally,
Subsection 2.4 identifies the research gap this study aims to address.
2.1. Crash Prediction Models
The first studies in RTCP focus on highways using statistical and machine learning methods. Hossain
and Muromachi (2012) proposed a hybrid model that combines multinomial logit regression with Bayesian
Belief Networks to identify key predictors and forecast collisions on the Tokyo Metropolitan Expressway.
Basso et al. (2018) developed a Support Vector Machine model to analyze factors associated with accidents
on the Autopista Central in Santiago, Chile. Expanding on this work, Basso et al. (2021b) introduced a
deep learning model that uses vehicle-level data and image-based architectures such as CNN and GAN to
capture real-time driving patterns on the same expressway. More recently, Kabir and Roy (2023) applied a
log-logistic model to Indian Ministry of Transport data, highlighting the predictive value of traffic density,
prior accidents, weather, and time of day.
Crash prediction at urban intersections and segments has recently received growing attention, driven
by the increasing availability of detailed traffic data (Palit and Osman, 2023). These locations often
experience a high number of collisions due to their structural complexity and dynamic traffic patterns.
Alajali et al. (2018) proposed a model for intersections in Victoria, Australia, combining crash reports,
roadworks, and environmental data within an ensemble decision tree framework. In Canada, Zheng and
Sayed (2020) developed a real-time prediction model for signalized intersections using the Generalized
Extreme Value (GEV) distribution, a method commonly used in intersection crash analysis due to its
ability to model rare and extreme events. More recent studies have incorporated data from connected
vehicles. For example, Gu et al. (2023) applied a Geographical Random Forest to large-scale datasets
from the Safety Pilot Model Deployment in Ann Arbor, USA. Wang et al. (2025) introduced RoadInTCP,
a deep learning model for sequential, multimodal crash prediction at intersections, which was validated
using traffic data from New York City.
ITS increasingly incorporate predictive modeling to enhance road safety and support operational
decision-making (Elassy et al., 2024; Elamrani Abou Elassad et al., 2025). Within this framework, predictive models provide critical capabilities for identifying high-risk conditions, guiding infrastructure investment, and improving emergency preparedness. For example, Edward (2024) developed an intelligent
accident crisis management system that combines Modified Convolutional Neural Networks (MCNN) with
Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to improve both crash detection and response efficiency through
vehicle trajectory analysis. Similarly, Audu et al. (2021) and Kepaptsoglou et al. (2012) examined the
integration of machine learning into traffic emergency management frameworks, emphasizing its potential
to optimize real-time responses and allocate resources more effectively in transportation systems.
However, as predictive models become increasingly embedded into real-time traffic management and
decision-support systems, a key operational challenge remains unresolved: how to ensure their sustained
performance in the face of evolving traffic dynamics. Most existing implementations rely on static or periodically updated models, often lacking mechanisms to detect and respond to changes in data distributions.
This shortcoming limits their long-term effectiveness, particularly in non-stationary environments such as
urban highways or intersections, where traffic patterns, behaviors, and risks can shift rapidly. As noted in
Shirazi et al. (2016), even the calibration factors used in standardized safety models can become unreliable
over time if not regularly updated with sufficient data and methodological rigor.
3

2.2. Recalibration
Machine learning models deployed in real-world applications, such as traffic prediction or real-time
flow monitoring, face the ongoing challenge of maintaining performance over time (Wang and Machida,
2024). A common approach to address this issue is recalibration, which involves periodically updating
or retraining the model using new data samples (Wu et al., 2020). Recalibration typically depends on
prediction quality and observed data shifts, enabling the model to adapt as conditions change (Casimiro
et al., 2022), or is triggered after a sufficient volume of new data becomes available (Kavikondala et al.,
2019).
One of the main drivers behind the need for recalibration is the emergence of changes in the data known
as drift, that is, alterations in the statistical distribution of the data over time (Tantithamthavorn et al.,
2016). These changes are inevitable in dynamic environments such as urban traffic, sensor monitoring,
or financial systems, and may manifest in different forms. The first and most common is data drift, also
referred to as covariate shift (Sugiyama et al., 2007), which occurs when the marginal distribution of the
input variables changes without necessarily altering their relationship with the target variable.
On the other hand, within the domain of drift, concept drift emerges as a critical phenomenon in
machine learning systems deployed in dynamic environments, where the distribution of input data evolves
over time, negatively impacting predictive performance (Shang et al., 2025). This issue is particularly
relevant in applications that process real-time data streams, such as traffic prediction, fraud detection, or
sensor analytics in IoT environments (Yang and Shami, 2021). To address this challenge, several methodologies have been developed to detect and adapt models to these changes, preserving their generalization
capability.
Seminal works in this field laid the foundation for modern approaches. Widmer and Kubat (1996)
introduced the FLORA algorithm, a rule-based incremental learning method for binary classification
with nominal data, designed to handle concept drift. It models each concept through three sets of rule
antecedents: ADES (positive examples), NDES (negative examples), and PDES (potential descriptions
matching both classes). ADES classifies new data, NDES prevents overgeneralization, and PDES stores
descriptions that may become relevant later, with counters tracking coverage in a sliding window. Gama
et al. (2004) introduce a pioneering technique based on monitoring the model’s online error rate. By
detecting statistically significant deviations in this rate, the system can infer that a shift in the underlying
distribution has occurred and act accordingly. This method proved especially effective in online learning
scenarios, where immediate feedback enables timely adaptation.
Subsequently, Bifet and Gavalda (2007) developed one of the most influential algorithms to date:
ADWIN (Adaptive Windowing). This detector dynamically adjusts the size of a sliding window over the
data stream to identify concept changes without requiring a fixed window size or a predefined threshold.
Its computational efficiency, ease of implementation, and effectiveness in non-stationary environments have
positioned it as a central reference in the drift detection literature. The importance of data drift detectors
has been further solidified through multiple contemporary validations. Around the same time, Spinosa
et al. (2007) proposed the OLINDDA algorithm (OnLIne Novelty and Drift Detection Algorithm), which
models the normal concept from a single class (one-class classification) and monitors unrecognized data
to detect the formation of new clusters.
In recent years, studies such as Pinagé et al. (2020), Souza et al. (2020), Korycki and Krawczyk (2021),
Micevska et al. (2021), and Rabinovich et al. (2023) have shown that these methods not only preserve
predictive performance but also minimize computational overhead and reduce the need for frequent retraining. This progress makes them particularly valuable in applications where computational resources
are limited or latency is critical.

4

2.3. Implementation of Drift Detection in Transport
While concept and data drift detection techniques have seen rapid development in domains such as
finance and e-commerce, their deployment in transportation applications is comparatively recent. A
growing body of work now demonstrates how adaptive drift-handling mechanisms can preserve model
performance and resilience within highly dynamic mobility environments.
Early real-time traffic studies illustrated the value of built-in adaptation. For instance, Wibisono et al.
(2016) employed FIMT-DD, an incremental model tree with native drift detection, to forecast large-scale
highway flow in the UK, maintaining accuracy under temporal shifts. Similarly, Saadallah et al. (2018)
developed the BRIGHT ensemble for short-term taxi demand forecasting across Portugal, China, and
Sweden, integrating drift-aware base learners and bias correction to respond to rapidly changing urban
conditions. Subsequent work extended adversarial and transport-theoretic approaches. Pan et al. (2020)
combined adversarial validation with gradient-boosted decision trees to detect and correct distributional
shifts in Uber user targeting across the Asia-Pacific region. Laha and Verma (2021) introduced OTDD,
using Optimal Transport distances to identify drift in mobility mode classification using smartphone and
vehicular sensor streams.
In continuous stream and edge/fog settings, adaptive windowing and unsupervised signal-based detectors have proven effective. Manias et al. (2021) used an auto-tuned ensemble (PWPAE) on a MNISTderived vehicular traffic simulation to showcase windowed drift adaptation, while Andresini et al. (2021) applied similar window-based strategies for network traffic streams in edge/fog infrastructures. Malekghaini
et al. (2022) further demonstrated spectral-entropy drift detection on real-world transport network communications, highlighting the practicality of unsupervised methods in monitoring high-volume data.
Drift detection has also been explored in autonomous vehicle control and encrypted traffic analytics.
Lee and Park (2022) proposed the OSWDD sliding-window detector alongside regression models to adapt
to non-stationary conditions in simulated AV systems. Malekghaini et al. (2023) investigated robustness
of CNN/LSTM classifiers under encrypted mobile-traffic drift by combining drift-impact analysis with
robust re-tuning strategies. In safety-critical environments, Hossain et al. (2024) examined data drift
effects on crash-risk and traffic prediction using multi-sensor fusion (camera, LiDAR, radar), highlighting
the operational risks of unaddressed drift in vehicle safety systems.
Building on these advances, the present work implements ADWIN, a statistically principled adaptive
windowing detector, to enable automated recalibration of batch-trained neural networks for RTCP from
AVI sensor streams.
2.4. Research gap
Based on the analysis of recent literature, a sustained evolution can be observed in crash prediction
models applied to highways, which have increasingly incorporated advanced technologies such as deep
learning architectures, spatial networks, and connected vehicle data. However, a critically underexplored
dimension concerns the recalibration of predictive models in non-stationary environments. Specifically,
there is a notable lack of studies that clearly define when recalibration should occur, whether periodically
or through post-hoc strategies triggered by model performance or composite metrics. Most current studies
assume static models or defer recalibration to manual or heuristic processes, lacking systematic criteria
or empirical validation regarding their timing, frequency, or effectiveness. Instead, they typically evaluate
supervised model performance over evolving datasets without addressing distributional changes.
Despite recent advances, most applications to date have focused on traffic flow, demand forecasting,
user profiling, or mobility analytics, often relying on synthetic or narrowly scoped datasets. As illustrated
in Table 1, concept and data drift have been explored in various transportation contexts, from urban
mobility to autonomous vehicles and transportation networks. However, the explicit use of drift detection
as a principled trigger for model recalibration remains virtually unexplored in real-world crash prediction

scenarios. Notably, none of the studies reviewed address real-time recalibration for crash risk estimation
using highway-level operational data.
Therefore, the present study directly addresses this gap by systematically comparing several recalibration strategies, static, periodic, and cumulative retraining, against an adaptive recalibration framework
guided by concept drift detection via the ADWIN algorithm. These strategies are evaluated in terms of

3. Data Description
To train the predictive models for RTCP, we use multiple data sources provided by the Autopista
Central in Chile. The study period spans from January 1, 2018 to September 30, 2024, covering the full
24-hour cycle between 00:00 and 23:59 each day. First, in Subsection 3.1, we describe the traffic flow data

captured by the AVI gates. Then, in Subsection 3.2, we provide a description of the manually recorded

3.1. AVI Data
The data used in this study was provided by Autopista Central in Santiago, Chile, a major highway
running north–south across the Metropolitan Region. It spans 62.3 kilometers and connects the Américo
Vespucio ring road with the Maipo River. The infrastructure consists of two main segments: the General
Velásquez branch and the Ruta 5 highway. This study focuses exclusively on the Ruta 5 segment (see
Figure 1).
The analysis is based on an extensive dataset of vehicular traffic collected through the network of AVI’s
installed along the highway. These sensors operate continuously and automatically, recording individual
vehicle passages and generating detailed, high-frequency traffic data. In total, 3,618,662,236 records were
obtained, each corresponding to a single vehicle crossing an AVI gate. Each row in the dataset includes
attributes such as timestamp, speed, license plate, gate ID, lane number, and vehicle type. Vehicles are
categorized into three classes: light, heavy, and motorcycle (see Table 2).

3.2. Accident Data
Autopista Central has provided a total of 9,984 accident records corresponding to the period between
January 2018 and September 2024. This database constitutes the official source of incident events on the
roadway and is manually recorded by the concessionaire’s operational staff. Each accident is documented
with three key attributes: the date and time of the incident, the segment of the highway where it occurred,
and the corresponding roadway, specifying whether the event took place in the Eastbound or Westbound
direction. This information enables both spatial and temporal georeferencing of each event, allowing
integration with traffic flow data. For the purposes of this study, we focus on the previously described
segment of the highway, which accounts for a total of 1,152 recorded accidents.
The hourly distribution of accidents in the previously mentioned segment reveals a significant concentration between 12:00 and 14:59, the time window with the highest percentage of accidents. Specifically,
the intervals from 12:00–12:59 and 13:00–13:59 account for 8.7% and 8.1% of the total number of accidents,

4. Methodology
This section presents the methodology implemented to develop and evaluate RTCP models, including
a description of concept drift and the recalibration strategies used to maintain model performance over
time. The structure is organized into seven main components. First, in Subsection 4.1, we present the
feature engineering process, in which explanatory variables are constructed from raw data, including transformations and aggregations. Then, in Subsection 4.2, we describe the data preparation process, including

4.2. Data preparation
The literature has shown that the presence of incomplete or inconsistent records can introduce significant biases and negatively impact the performance of discriminative models (Rahm and Do, 2000; García
et al., 2016). Therefore, a rigorous cleaning procedure was implemented, which included the removal of
variables with a high percentage of missing data, the exclusion of intervals with incomplete observations,
and the filtering of extended periods without recorded accidents potentially attributable to failures in the
data capture infrastructure.
The data preparation process, presented in Figure 4, involved two critical stages to ensure the reliability
of the dataset for predictive modeling. First, a rigorous treatment of missing data was applied. Variables
with more than 1% of missing values were removed to avoid biased imputations or significant row loss.
This led to the exclusion of 17 out of 221 original variables, retaining a total of 204 predictive features.
Additionally, all time intervals containing any missing value across the remaining variables were discarded,
reducing the number of valid observations from 687,456 to 683,701.
Secondly, a temporal filtering step was implemented to address sequences of several consecutive days
with no recorded accidents. These anomalous windows, particularly those lasting seven days or more,
raised concerns about potential failures in the data acquisition system, especially when coinciding with
irregularities in AVI sensor activity. As a preventive measure, all affected periods were removed to avoid
introducing artificial patterns into the models. In total, 420 days were excluded from the dataset, with
a stronger impact during 2019 and 2020. After applying both cleaning procedures, the final dataset
comprised 563,518 five-minute intervals and 1,101 recorded accidents.

4.3.1. Correlation Matrix
The first stage of variable selection involves detecting and removing highly correlated variables in
order to reduce redundancy and avoid multicollinearity issues. This analysis was applied exclusively to
the training set corresponding to the year 2018, maintaining the independence of the test set and preventing
data leakage.
Out of the 204 predictive variables remaining after filtering for missing data, pairwise collinearity was
assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient ρxy . This metric quantifies the linear relationship between
two continuous variables. Given the high number of features, the procedure was automated, and variables
with a correlation greater than 0.95 with at least one other variable in the set were removed. This threshold
is commonly used in the literature to identify strong relationships that do not add additional value to the
model and may induce overfitting or instability in parameter estimation (Wu et al., 2019). Eliminating
these variables improves model interpretability and significantly reduces dimensionality without substantial
loss of information.
This step not only enhances model interpretability but also reduces dimensionality and mitigates the
risk of overfitting during training. A total of 38 highly correlated variables with a coefficient ρxy > 0.95
relative to other variables in the set were removed leaving us with 166 variables with no high correlation.
Figure 5 shows the density distribution of the absolute values of the pairwise correlation coefficients (|ρij |)
among all predictive variables in the training set. Most variable pairs exhibit very low correlation, while
only a small proportion exceed the cutoff of 0.95 (indicated by the red dashed line). The cutoff selected
is based on Basso et al. (2020). This justifies the choice of the threshold and illustrates that the majority
of variables provide complementary information, with only a limited number of highly redundant features
that were systematically excluded from further analysis.

4.3.2. Random Forest for feature selection
After eliminating highly correlated variables, an additional feature selection step was carried out using
a Random Forest model, an approach widely recognized for its ability to identify relevant predictive
variables in nonlinear contexts with complex interactions (Abdel-Aty et al., 2008; Ahmed and Abdel-Aty,
2011; Xu et al., 2012; Basso et al., 2018).
The Random Forest algorithm operates by constructing an ensemble of decision trees using bootstrap
samples of the original dataset. At each split in a tree, a random subset of predictors is evaluated
to determine the optimal split point, using the Gini impurity as the criterion. This mechanism not only
enhances the diversity among trees but also provides a robust framework for assessing variable importance.
Specifically, the importance of each predictor is quantified by the average decrease in Gini impurity across
all trees when that variable is used for splitting. Furthermore, the model’s generalization capacity is
evaluated using Out-of-Bag (OOB) samples, those not included in each bootstrap iteration, allowing
performance metrics such as accuracy and AUC to be estimated without the need for a separate validation
set.
The final Random Forest model was trained on a total of 75,917 observations from 2018, using 166
predictive variables, and performing binary classification between intervals with and without accidents.
During the calibration process, different values of the complexity parameter mtry were tested, with the
best performance obtained at mtry = 84, achieving an average AUC of 0.584, perfect sensitivity (1.00),
and zero specificity (0.00). Although this combination reveals a strong tendency of the model to predict
the majority class, our primary objective was to use this structure to estimate the relative importance
of each predictor. This estimation was based on the average decrease in impurity between classes, which
enabled the generation of a detailed ranking of predictor variable relevance.
Based on this ranking, the 15 most important variables were selected for the accident prediction task,
prioritizing those with the highest discriminative power. Among these are, for example, spatial differences
in heavy vehicle flow (Flow.15.14.Heavy), lane-specific speed variations (Vel.15.14.Lane2), absolute car
speed (Vel.Cars.14), and speed dispersion (SD.Cars.14). Figure 6 shows the top 20 variables with the

4.4. Drift
In the context of machine learning and predictive modeling, the term drift refers to the variability or
change in data distributions over time (Lu et al., 2018). This phenomenon can compromise a model’s predictive capability, as the patterns observed during training may become obsolete as underlying conditions
change. Broadly speaking, there are two main types of drift: data drift and concept drift. To address
these challenges, several drift detection algorithms have been proposed, with ADWIN being one of the
most widely adopted due to its theoretical guarantees and ability to adjust dynamically to changes without requiring parameter tuning for the window size. Comparative studies such as those by Gonçalves Jr
et al. (2014); Barros and Santos (2018) have highlighted ADWIN’s consistent performance across multiple
scenarios, particularly in streaming and highly imbalanced environments. Furthermore, de Barros and
de Carvalho Santos (2019) emphasizes its utility as a lightweight and efficient component within ensemble frameworks for non-stationary data, making it a suitable candidate for applications like ours where
real-time recalibration and computational feasibility are critical.
Data drift (also known as covariate shift) occurs when the distributions of input predictors (explanatory variables) change over time, while the relationship between these attributes and the target variable
(dependent variable) remains constant. In other words, the model continues to map inputs to outputs in
the same way, but the nature of the inputs themselves evolves, diverging from the distributions observed
during the training phase. This phenomenon often leads to a degradation in model performance, as the
system is no longer exposed to familiar patterns. Data drift is particularly prevalent in real-time systems
that process continuous data, which is often affected by dynamic contextual conditions such as traffic or
weather. These systems are particularly susceptible to variations in inputs due to seasonality, infrastructure
changes, or external disruptions. The first studies in the field of drift emerged as machine-learning-based
decision support systems began to be deployed in these types of non-stationary environments. Notably,
the works of Gama et al. (2004) and Bifet and Gavalda (2007) were pioneering in recognizing drift as
18

a critical phenomenon and proposing adaptive mechanisms for maintaining model relevance in changing
data conditions.
Concept drift, on the other hand, refers to a change in the relationship between input predictors and
the target variable (Gama et al., 2014). In other words, the model can no longer make accurate predictions
because the concept or nature of the output data has changed. This type of drift is significantly more
problematic, as not only have the input data changed, but also the underlying structure that links the
predictors with the target variable of interest (Webb et al., 2016).
The main difference between data drift and concept drift lies in which component of the data distribution changes over time. Data drift refers to changes in the marginal distribution of the input features,
that is, P (X), without altering the conditional relationship between these features and the target variable.
This occurs, for example, when the types or frequencies of inputs the model receives change, such as shifts
in traffic volume, weather patterns, or road usage, while the underlying relationship between inputs and
outcomes remains stable. Since P (X) captures how the input data is distributed, any variation in context, behavior, or environment that modifies the frequency or nature of inputs constitutes data drift. In
contrast, concept drift refers to changes in the conditional distribution P (Y | X), meaning that even if the
inputs remain the same, the way they relate to the output changes over time. This reflects an evolution in
the underlying data-generating process that governs the target variable. In the context of crash prediction,
for instance, concept drift may arise when external factors, such as changes in driver behavior, enforcement
policies, or road infrastructure, alter the risk dynamics associated with specific traffic patterns. Because
P (Y | X) directly defines the model’s decision function, concept drift represents a more fundamental shift
that cannot be corrected by reweighting inputs alone and typically requires retraining or adaptive learning
mechanisms (Lu et al., 2018; Žliobaitė et al., 2016; Bayram et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2023).
To address the phenomenon of concept drift in online data environments, several strategies have been
proposed, among which the ADWIN (Adaptive Windowing) algorithm introduced by Bifet and Gavalda
(2007) stands out. This algorithm enables the real-time detection of concept changes by continuously
monitoring the mean within an adaptive sliding window. As new data is incorporated, ADWIN evaluates
potential partitions of the current window and automatically reduces its size if statistically significant differences are detected between the subsets, indicating a change in the distribution. The detailed procedure
of this approach is summarized in Algorithm 1.

4.5. Prediction Models
In this section, we describe the models that are used in the next experiments
XGBoost: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), developed by Chen and Guestrin (2016), is a
decision tree-based model that learns sequentially, improving with each iteration by focusing on the errors
made in previous iterations. Its design makes it particularly efficient and accurate, and it is widely used in
data science due to its ability to handle nonlinear relationships and large datasets, with several successful
applications in the transportation domain (Basso et al., 2022a).
AdaBoost: Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) (Schapire and Singer, 1998) combines multiple simple
models, known as weak learners, into a strong classifier. In each round, it shifts attention to the errors
made by the previous model, assigning more weight to the misclassified observations.
Random Forest: Random Forest (Breiman, 2001) builds multiple decision trees using random subsets
of data and variables, then combines their predictions to make a final decision. This approach helps reduce
overfitting and improves the model’s generalization ability.
Neural Network (NNet): Neural networks (Rumelhart et al., 1986) are models inspired by the
human brain. Through layers of interconnected nodes, they can learn complex patterns in data. In this
study, a simple multilayer neural network is used to classify binary outcomes, leveraging its ability to
model nonlinear relationships among variables.
The decision threshold was not fixed at the conventional value of 0.5, but was instead dynamically
adjusted for each model-year combination through an internal validation process. Specifically, in each
training instance, 20% of the training data was set aside as a stratified validation set. On this partition, the
predicted probabilities from the model were computed, and the corresponding ROC curve was constructed.
From this curve, the optimal decision threshold was identified using the Youden criterion (Fluss et al.,
2005), defined as:

4.7. Experimental design
The experimental design of this study aims to evaluate the generalization capability and robustness of
various binary classification models for predicting the occurrence of accidents in 5-minute intervals.
• No recalibration: In this scenario, a single model is trained using data from the year 2018 and is
applied without any adjustments to predict each of the subsequent years (2019–2024). This approach
allows for the evaluation of the model’s generalization ability in the face of temporal changes without
any updates.
• Recalibration using the previous year (period-aligned retraining ): For each evaluation year
t, a new model is trained using only the data from year t − 1 . This strategy captures recent traffic
and accident dynamics, annually recalibrating the model with the most recent available information.
• Cumulative recalibration: A model is trained using all available data from 2018 up to the year
immediately before prediction (≤ t−1). This approach uses the accumulation of historical experience,
allowing the model to incorporate long-term trends.
• Adaptive retraining with concept drift detection (ADWIN): In this experiment, the ADWIN
algorithm is used to detect changes in the distribution of prediction errors in real time, applying an
automatic recalibration approach. The procedure starts with training a base model on 2018 data.
Then, sequential prediction is performed data point by data point (5 minutes period) for the period
following 2018. After each prediction, it is checked whether the model made an error (1 if incorrect,
0 if correct), and this binary indicator is passed to the ADWIN algorithm. If no change is detected in
the error distribution, predictions continue using the current model. If drift is detected, the change
point is recorded, relevant metrics are computed, and the model is retrained using only the new
adaptive window W1 identified by ADWIN (xt in Algorithm 1). This procedure enables continuous
and automatic adaptation to conceptual changes in the data, without requiring explicit definition
of recalibration instances. In our implementation, we set the ADWIN confidence parameter to
δ = 0.002, the value recommended by Bifet and Gavalda (2007) as a good balance between falsealarm rate and drift sensitivity, and required a minimum window size of Wmin = 45,000 observations
so that every retraining step would draw on a sufficiently large sample to stabilise gradient-based
learners in this highly imbalanced, non-stationary setting.
Each of these strategies was applied to four classification algorithms widely used in the accident prediction literature: XGBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest and neural networks (NNet). For all experimental
strategies, the same training and validation procedures were applied, based on 5-fold stratified crossvalidation.

pandemic, when training on the previous year (e.g., 2019 for 2020 predictions) leads to marked declines
in sensitivity for XGBoost and AdaBoost. The lowest AUCs for both models under this strategy occur in
2021, when models were retrained on 2020 data.
Although designed to respond dynamically to data changes, ADWIN-based recalibration generally
yields the lowest AUCs in three of the four models (XGBoost, Random Forest, and NNet). Its adaptivity
appears insufficient to compensate for the gains offered by cumulative learning, likely due to delayed or
overly frequent drift detections that fragment training data or miss relevant distributional shifts. Despite its computational efficiency, ADWIN’s performance limitations reduce its reliability in real-world
applications.
We now dig further into the performance of each of the four predictive models.
• XGBoost remains the top-performing model overall, particularly under cumulative strategies. Its
AUC scores frequently exceed 0.70, reaching a peak of 0.7637 in 2023, while sensitivity improves
with larger datasets, reaching 0.7761 in 2024. These gains, however, come at the cost of high
computational time (284.23 minutes in the cumulative setup) (Table A.8). Under ADWIN, XGBoost
achieves the lowest performance in terms of AUC, highlighting the trade-off between responsiveness
and accuracy.
• AdaBoost offers a strong balance between accuracy and efficiency, achieving AUCs comparable
to XGBoost while reducing training time by 75%. It performs best under the cumulative strategy
(average AUC = 0.711) and shows notable adaptability in the ADWIN setup, where it slightly
outperforms XGBoost in AUC. In 2024, adaptive recalibration enables AdaBoost to recover from
earlier declines, achieving its highest AUC (0.7391) and sensitivity (0.65), demonstrating the value
of local responsiveness (Table A.9).
• Random Forest performs consistently worse than XGBoost and AdaBoost, though ADWIN yields
better relative performance for this model (AUC = 0.664). While it occasionally reaches high AUCs
(up to 0.7555) and sensitivity above 0.62, it often displays a conservative bias, favoring the majority
class. This is reflected in high specificity but low sensitivity, especially under static and adaptive
strategies.
• NNet consistently underperformed across all strategies, achieving its best results only under cumulative recalibration (peak AUC: 0.6915 in 2023, sensitivity: 0.7687 in 2024). Three key limitations
emerged: (1) severe overfitting to the majority class, evidenced by persistent high-confidence false
negatives; (2) minimal benefit from adaptive recalibration, with ADWIN-based AUC fluctuating
erratically between 0.46-0.65; and (3) deteriorating computational efficiency, with training times
increasing six-fold from 10 to 62 minutes.

24

While ADWIN offers theoretical advantages for handling concept drift, its effectiveness drops in highly
imbalanced contexts like crash prediction, where the positive class accounts for only 0.2% of the data.
In such settings, the algorithm often produces false drift signals driven by fluctuations in the majority
class, reducing model stability. The need for repeated retraining and relabeling further prevents models
from learning long-term patterns, as shown by the 6–8 deviation detections per model reported in Table
A.9. These limitations point to the need for domain-specific drift detection methods better aligned with
traffic safety applications. Similar shortcomings of ADWIN have also been observed in other areas, such
as document classification (Kronberg, 2024).
6. Concluding Remarks
Accurate crash prediction is essential for improving road safety, yet maintaining model performance
over time remains a challenge due to changing traffic conditions and data distributions. Previous research
has advanced a variety of predictive models, from statistical and machine learning approaches to modern
deep learning methods, and has explored recalibration techniques to counter performance degradation.
However, most studies have focused on single-year evaluations or limited update strategies, leaving open
questions about how different recalibration methods perform in real-world, multi-year contexts. To address
this gap, we systematically evaluated four recalibration strategies: static, period-aligned, cumulative, and
adaptive (via ADWIN); applied to RTCP models (XGBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest, and NNet) using
vehicle-level data from the Autopista Central in Santiago, Chile. Our analysis compares these strategies
across multiple years, providing empirical evidence on their impact on predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and
computational efficiency in a dynamic traffic environment.
The findings indicate that ensemble-based methods, particularly XGBoost and AdaBoost, consistently
achieved the highest predictive performance. Of these two methods, XGBoost achived superior overall
accuracy and sensitivity, while AdaBoost provided comparable predictive performance with 75% shorter
training times. The cumulative strategy proved most effective overall, consistently delivering the highest
AUC values (0.711 for XGBoost and AdaBoost) by using of historical data to capture long-term traffic
patterns. In contrast, adaptive recalibration with ADWIN performed poorly, hindered by unstable drift
detections and large sensitivity fluctuations that limited its practical value.
A key finding is the sharp performance drop in some cases when models were recalibrated with only
the preceding year’s data, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. Training on 2019 data to predict
2020 resulted in AUC drops due to the model’s inability to capture pandemic-induced traffic changes,
including lockdowns and altered commuting patterns, affected commuting patterns, and fluctuations in
vehicle flow. This led to poor generalization, reduced accuracy, and unstable predictions, showing the risk
of short-term, period-aligned retraining in volatile contexts. In contrast, cumulative recalibration, while
more computationally demanding, used long-term historical data to capture broader trends, producing
more stable and accurate results across changing traffic conditions.
Another important result is the consistently weak performance of NNet under all strategies, marked
by low sensitivity and frequent high-confidence misclassifications —an operational risk in safety-critical
systems. Random Forest was the only model whose ADWIN-based results approached those of cumulative
training, showing some robustness while maintaining moderate training times, making it a viable option
when computational resources are limited. Overall, the results point to ensemble methods such as XGBoost
and AdaBoost as the most stable and adaptable, with fewer high-confidence errors across strategies.
Taken together, these findings provide practical guidelines for the operation of RTCP systems. Among
them, cumulative recalibration stands out as the most robust and accurate strategy, as it uses historical
information to maintain model stability and outperform both static schemes and period-aligned recalibration, the latter proving fragile in the face of abrupt structural changes (e.g., COVID-19). Furthermore,

25

the results suggest that the use of drift detectors such as ADWIN should not be aimed at replacing cumulative recalibration, but rather at complementing it in operational practice: its greatest value lies in
monitoring online error and triggering review or retraining alerts under persistent conditions of change,
thereby functioning as an adaptive surveillance mechanism.
Some limitations should be acknowledged. This study examined only four predictive models (XGBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest, NNet) and four recalibration strategies (static, period-aligned, cumulative, adaptive via ADWIN). Other modeling approaches, update methods, and concept drift detection
algorithms were not explored and may perform better in some settings. The results also reflect a specific
case: crash prediction on Chile’s Autopista Central, a tolled urban highway with free-flow traffic. Infrastructure characteristics, local driving behavior, and seasonal patterns, including national holidays, may
differ elsewhere, so the generalization of these findings to other regions or traffic systems should be made
with caution.
This study points to several next steps. One is to test recalibration methods that take into account
traffic cycles and seasons, since patterns often repeat by time of day, day of week, or month. Training
models with data from the same calendar period could improve accuracy, especially in Chile where crashes
peak every September during national holidays. Another step is to design drift detection and recalibration
methods better suited to local traffic instead of only using general tools like ADWIN. Finally, combining
adaptive drift detection with cumulative retraining may help balance quick reactions to changes with
learning from long-term patterns. Overall, the results show that well-planned recalibration can help keep
crash prediction systems reliable over time.
